Ethical Considerations

Responsible use of student data and predictive analytics

Ethical Responsibility

The project recognizes the substantial ethical responsibilities involved in managing student data and generating risk classifications. Because the dashboard uses academic, attendance, and financial indicators to estimate student engagement risk, it must be implemented in ways that protect privacy, minimize harm, and promote equity across all student groups.

Core Ethical Principles

🔒

Privacy Protection

All student data must be handled with strict confidentiality, following FERPA regulations and implementing role-based access controls to ensure only authorized personnel can view sensitive information.

⚖️

Fairness & Equity

Regular audits of the risk assessment algorithm to identify and mitigate potential biases that could disproportionately affect marginalized student populations.

🎯

Support, Not Stigma

Risk scores should guide supportive interventions, never punitive measures. Students should not be informed of their numerical risk score to prevent stigmatization.

🔍

Transparency

Clear communication about what data is collected, how it's used, and how decisions are made, ensuring institutional accountability and trust.

Key Ethical Considerations

1. Privacy and Data Protection

The system is designed with FERPA principles as a foundational requirement.

  • Access Controls: Role-based access control (RBAC) ensures only authorized advisors, department chairs, and administrators can view student data appropriate to their role
  • Data Minimization: The system collects only the data necessary for risk assessment and advising, avoiding unnecessary sensitive attributes
  • De-identification: For external reporting and research purposes, data must be aggregated and de-identified to protect individual privacy
  • Audit Trails: All data access is logged to ensure accountability and detect unauthorized access attempts

2. Algorithmic Bias and Fairness

The risk scoring model must be continuously evaluated for unintended reinforcement of inequities.

  • GPA & Attendance Bias: The high weighting of GPA (40%) and attendance (30%) may disadvantage students with disabilities, chronic health conditions, or complex family responsibilities that affect their ability to attend consistently
  • Financial Component Concerns: The 10% financial weight may disproportionately identify low-income students as higher risk, potentially leading to surveillance rather than support
  • Contextual Consideration: The score should prompt advisors to explore context, not serve as a definitive label. A student with low attendance due to documented disability accommodations should not be automatically flagged as high-risk
  • Regular Audits: Periodic analysis by demographic groups (race, socioeconomic status, first-generation status) to ensure the model doesn't systematically disadvantage certain populations

3. Supportive vs. Punitive Use

Critical implementation guidelines to prevent misuse:

  • No Direct Student Disclosure: Students should NOT be informed of their numerical risk score. Instead, outreach should focus on offering support and encouragement
  • Proactive Support: Risk flags should trigger offers of tutoring, counseling, financial aid counseling, or peer mentoring—never academic probation or enrollment restrictions without due process
  • Advisor Training: Advisors must be trained to use the dashboard as a conversation starter, not a judgment tool. Cultural competency training is essential
  • Student Agency: Students should have the right to decline support services without penalty

4. Data Security

Protection against breaches and unauthorized access:

  • Encrypted data storage and transmission (SSL/TLS)
  • Secure authentication (JWT tokens, password hashing)
  • Regular security audits and penetration testing
  • Incident response plan for potential data breaches
  • Compliance with institutional data governance policies

Frequently Asked Questions

Can students see their own risk scores? +

No. Students should not have direct access to their numerical risk scores to prevent stigmatization and anxiety. Instead, advisors reach out proactively with supportive messaging focused on available resources and opportunities for improvement.

How do we prevent algorithmic bias? +

Regular audits analyzing risk score distribution across demographic groups, continuous model refinement based on outcome data, and mandatory contextual review by human advisors before any intervention. The algorithm is a tool to prompt investigation, not make final decisions.

What happens to student data after graduation? +

Data retention policies should align with FERPA and institutional guidelines. Typically, academic records are maintained indefinitely for transcript purposes, but risk assessment data and advisor notes may be archived or de-identified after a set period (e.g., 5 years post-graduation).

Who has access to the dashboard? +

Access is strictly controlled through role-based permissions: Academic advisors see their assigned students, department chairs see departmental aggregates, financial aid officers see relevant financial data, and institutional leadership sees anonymized institutional metrics. All access is logged for audit purposes.

Technology Serves Students

The dashboard exists to support student success, not to surveil or punish. Ethical implementation requires continuous vigilance, training, and a commitment to equity.
